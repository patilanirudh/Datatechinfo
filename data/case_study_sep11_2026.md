## What happened

Friday, September 11, 2026 was an ordinary workday on the calendar but not in practice: September 12 fell on a second Saturday (a standard Karnataka government holiday), September 13 was a Sunday, and September 14 was Ganesh Chaturthi (Gauri-Ganesha) — a major festival most people spend at their family home. That combination created a four-day window, and hundreds of thousands of Bengaluru residents tried to leave the city on the same Friday evening.

From around 5 PM onward, KSRTC and private bus terminals — Kempegowda Bus Station (Majestic), Mysore Road Satellite Bus Station, and Shantinagar — were overwhelmed. Every major road leading out of the city gridlocked well into the night: Tumakuru Road, Mysore Road, Kanakapura Road, and Hosur Road. On the Bengaluru–Mysuru highway, queues stretched for kilometres at the Kaniminike and Sheshagirihalli toll plazas. A separate report documented a 4km+ jam on the Bengaluru–Nelamangala highway with ambulances reportedly stranded in the queue. Private bus operators raised fares 2–3x in response to the demand spike, drawing passenger complaints of price gouging. KSRTC added roughly 2,400 special buses to absorb the surge — extra capacity that still wasn't enough to prevent the gridlock, because the bottleneck was the road network and terminal capacity, not just seat availability.

This is the exact chain of events a passenger on a Bengaluru→Hubli VRL bus experienced first-hand: a bus scheduled for 10 PM arrived at 2 AM, a 4+ hour delay, sitting in the same jam described above.

## Why this keeps happening

This was not a one-off. The same failure pattern — same exit corridors, same chokepoints, same causes — recurs every time a multi-day festival or long weekend lines up in Bengaluru:

1. **Bengaluru starts with almost no slack.** It is the world's 2nd most congested city per TomTom's 2025 Traffic Index: 74.4% average congestion, 16.6 km/h average rush-hour speed (down from 18 km/h in 2024), and 168 hours lost per commuter per year. Any demand spike on top of that baseline has nowhere to absorb into.
2. **Demand concentrates into a single evening.** Most people leave after work on the last working day before the break, converging on the same handful of terminals and highways within a 3–4 hour window (roughly 5 PM–11 PM), rather than spreading across the day.
3. **The exodus itself creates secondary congestion.** Private vehicles ferrying people *to* the bus stands clog the surrounding city roads (Majestic, Malleswaram, Yeshwanthpur, Shantinagar), compounding the highway congestion beyond the terminals.
4. **Highway capacity bottlenecks at fixed points.** Toll plazas (Nelamangala, Kaniminike, Sheshagirihalli) become hard chokepoints under high volume regardless of how many buses are added — throughput is capped by lane count and toll processing speed, not fleet size.
5. **Added bus capacity doesn't fix a road-network bottleneck.** KSRTC's 2,400 extra buses addressed seat *supply*; the roads leading out of the city are still the same width. More buses on the same congested corridors can add to queue length rather than relieve it.

## Evidence this recurs year over year

Independent news coverage from prior years shows the identical pattern at the identical locations (Goraguntepalya Junction, Peenya flyover, the Nelamangala highway) ahead of Ganesh Chaturthi weekends in prior years — this is a structural, recurring failure mode of Bengaluru's transport network around clustered holidays, not a one-time incident.
